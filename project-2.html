01The shared experience

Our team built an application for remote, synchronous guidance between an expert and a novice inspecting a 3D CAD part. Each person places a copy of the part in their own space. The expert can turn it, draw guidance, mark problems with pins, and save a viewpoint for the novice to match.

The design explores how movement and spatial annotation can make guidance more active than watching a shared screen. A separate voice call can support the conversation; voice is not built into the application.

How to explore the demo
  1. Open the demo in two tabs or on two devices.
  2. Choose Expert in one and join as Novice in the other using its room code.
  3. On desktop, choose “Continue without AR” to explore the tools without camera access.
  4. Try Draw, then change your view and see how the guidance appears for the novice.

Phone AR requires camera permission. The team documents Chrome on a compatible Android device for WebXR, with partial iPhone support through Needle’s App Clip. Haptic feedback is unavailable on iPhone. These are project-specific support limits recorded in the team write-up.

02Finding a feasible
design direction

Our team first explored a mascot assistant that could mediate expert–novice communication through voice and visual cues. The sketches considered ways to explain a model, highlight questions, and convey expert feedback.

The team moved away from this direction because an assistant responding to such a broad set of inputs exceeded the available technical experience and time. We retained the goal of richer remote communication and explored using a phone’s movement to interact with a shared 3D space.

The resulting concept used the phone as a “ghost lens.” Spatial drawing, problem pins, and saved viewpoints could place guidance around the part itself. This concept was developed collectively; my individual work focused on implementing the expert interface and shared drawing.

EXPERT INTERFACE

Make the active tool legible.

The initial toolbar offered Grab, Pin, Draw, and View modes, with a crosshair and session status. The interface needed to communicate which action was active without taking over the model view.

Issue #7 implementation ↗
SHARED SKETCHING

Keep guidance in a shared frame.

Stroke points were expressed relative to the shared model space, allowing each device to place its own copy while exchanging the same guidance. Incoming points extended the novice’s drawing; completed strokes could be replayed on reconnection.

03What the review
revealed

After I submitted the drawing implementation, teammate review identified an important interaction conflict: selecting Draw should not prevent the expert from navigating around the part. The revision allowed background dragging and navigation modifiers to remain available while Draw was selected, reserving an unmodified drag on the part for sketching.

I also applied revisions to make the crosshair visually distinct, use the shared CSS colours, improve the drawing hint’s spacing, and consolidate the sketch input code. This work connected implementation details to how clearly the tool fits into the wider interface.

04 / VALIDATION & LIMITS

Check the feature.
Then the workflow.

I ran the implementation checks and production build, and manually tested expert and novice interactions in desktop browser tabs. My checks covered live strokes, synchronized clearing, reconnection replay, and switching modes. I also tested the requested revisions after review.

These desktop checks establish a narrower result than physical AR testing. They do not demonstrate phone tracking quality, spatial comfort, haptics, or whether novices learn more effectively. Physical-device testing was handled separately within the team.

A design question raised by this work is whether users can draw, inspect another angle, and continue guiding without losing their place. That is a useful next evaluation beyond checking that a stroke appears on both screens.

TEAM-IDENTIFIED NEXT STEPS

Make the workflow easier to enter
and easier to revisit.

Onboarding. Introduce the tools and expert–novice roles with a guided first session.

Richer annotation. Explore drawing controls, editable pin fixes, and previews of suggested changes.

Persistent guidance. Investigate saving and replaying sessions so novices can revisit explanations.

These are future directions documented in the team write-up, not completed features or measured outcomes.
